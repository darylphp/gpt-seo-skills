# Editorial Profile

IDs in brackets refer to the [source register](source-register.md). Text marked **Google guidance** paraphrases that source. Everything else is operating method.

## 1. What the profile is for

An editorial profile records how one brand writes for one market, so briefs and drafts don't guess. It is built from evidence the brand has approved and applied page by page through the brief's voice section. It is not a personality invented for the brand.

## 2. Building it (profile mode)

1. **List the sources.** For each, record what it is, who approved or supplied it, the date and what it can show. Approved style rules and past editorial corrections carry the most weight. Real published or approved pages show actual practice. Customer emails, reviews and call notes, used with permission, show the words readers use. Leave out competitor copy, lists of tone adjectives and AI-written descriptions of the brand, except as examples of what to avoid.
2. **Set the market.** Language variant, spelling, units and date style, from the brand's rules or market. Use Australian English when nothing else is set and the market doesn't need another variant.
3. **Describe the readers and their words.** Who reads, what they already know, what worries them and the words they use for the problem, from the audience sources. Note where the brand's terms differ from the readers'.
4. **Describe the tone with evidence.** For each trait, give a "Use" example from an approved sample and an "Avoid" example, as real sentences or close paraphrases with source IDs. A trait with no example is a guess: leave it out or record it as a question.
5. **Set the terms.** Preferred terms, terms to avoid, product and service names, capitalisation, and how numbers, prices and dates are written.
6. **Set authorship and point of view.** Who "we" is, when first person is allowed and for whom, how bylines and reviewer lines work, and what must never be implied without evidence, such as testing, local presence or a founder story.
7. **List what to avoid.** Prohibited claims as supplied by the brand or its regulators, phrases removed in past corrections, habits the samples show the brand dislikes, and the library defaults: no em dashes, no filler openings, no inflated adjectives, no closing paragraph that restates the page.
8. **Set formatting and CTA conventions.** Heading style, when lists and tables are used, and how CTAs are worded.
9. **Name the gaps.** What the sources don't settle, and the smallest question or sample that would settle each.

Ask at most three questions per attempt, and only where the answer would change a profile rule.

## 3. Applying it (draft mode)

The brief's voice section is the page-level instruction, and the profile supplies the detail. If they conflict, follow the brief and record the conflict in section 7 of the draft.

Check while drafting:

- Spelling, units, dates and number style match the market.
- Point of view matches the profile and the dossier's permissions.
- Terms come from the controlled list.
- The opening says something only this page could say.
- Each adjective is backed by evidence, or it goes.
- No sentence restates an earlier section's conclusion.
- Lists have as many items as the evidence supports, not three by habit.
- No em dashes. Use a comma, colon, full stop or brackets.

## 4. What natural writing is and isn't

Natural writing here means sentences a careful writer in the brand's market would write for this reader: specific, plain, accurate and in the approved register. It is a reader-quality goal. It is not evidence of human authorship or accuracy, and an AI-detector result, an "AI percentage" or a readability score is not an acceptance test.

**Google guidance [C01]:** Google's people-first questions include whether content has spelling or stylistic issues, whether headings exaggerate or shock, and whether content is mass-produced or spread across many sites. Its guidance on generative AI content asks for a focus on accuracy, quality and relevance [C02].
