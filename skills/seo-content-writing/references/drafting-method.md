# Drafting Method

IDs in brackets refer to the [source register](source-register.md). Text marked **Google guidance** paraphrases that source. Everything else is operating method.

## 1. Before the first sentence

Read the whole brief and dossier first. For each blueprint section, note its ID, purpose, reader need, key point, permitted claim IDs, qualifications, format, boundary and completion test. Note the do-not-use list, the link plan, the CTA concept, the approved assets and the voice rules.

Write a one-line plan for each section: what the reader learns or decides there, and which claims carry it. A section with no reader need goes back to the brief, not into the draft.

## 2. Order and placement

- Follow the blueprint order. Reorder only where the brief allows it, and record why in section 6.
- Answer the reader's main question near the top: what the page offers, and for whom.
- Put proof next to the claim it supports, not in a trust section far below.
- Put limitations and exclusions before the decision they affect, never after the CTA.
- Put the CTA after the reader has what they need for that step. Keep one primary CTA, and repeat it only where a reader would look for it.
- Keep topics another page owns to a short answer and a link, as the brief's boundary says.

## 3. Format follows the job

| What the reader is doing in the section | Format that usually helps |
| --- | --- |
| Comparing options on the same criteria | A table with units, criteria and the check date |
| Following an ordered process | Numbered steps |
| Checking whether something applies to them | A short list of conditions, or plain "suits" and "doesn't suit" lines |
| Weighing a reason or trade-off | Short paragraphs |
| Looking for one fact | A clear subheading with the fact in its first sentence |

Write headings that say what the section answers, and put no claim in a heading that the section doesn't support. Use an FAQ only for a question the brief or evidence shows readers ask, and the body doesn't already answer. Keep essential meaning in text, not only in an image, an accordion label or a table cell with no context.

**Google guidance [C13]:** Google's guide to its generative AI features says content should be organised for readers with headings that give a clear structure, and that there is no requirement to break content into tiny pieces or to write in a special way for those features.

## 4. Terms, queries and entities

Use the brief's query family and terminology rules to pick the words readers use, where they make a sentence clearer. Use each variant for its own meaning, not as a synonym rotated for search. Name the entities and criteria the reader's decision depends on.

Do not set or meet a keyword density, an exact-match count, an entity count, an optimisation tool score, a competitor's word count or a heading pattern copied from search results. If the user or a tool asks for one, say in section 6 that it was not applied and why.

**Google guidance [C01, C03]:** Google says it has no preferred word count, and lists writing to a particular word count as a sign of search-first content. Its spam policies give unnaturally repeated words and phrases, and blocks of text listing cities or regions, as examples of keyword stuffing.

## 5. Gaps, placeholders, notes and deviations

When a sentence the blueprint needs has no usable claim:

1. Use the dossier's approved narrower statement, if there is one.
2. Otherwise leave the sentence out, if the section still meets its completion test.
3. Otherwise write a numbered placeholder with the fact needed and its owner, such as `[Q1: confirm whether a callout fee applies, owner Mei Tan]`.

Never bridge a gap with a plausible fact, a typical figure, an industry average, a softened version of an unsupported claim, or phrases like "many customers say" or "experts agree". List every placeholder in section 6 with its owner and whether the page can go ahead without it.

A **note** is an observation for an owner that leaves no placeholder in the draft, such as a condition in the evidence that readers may find unclear. Notes don't stop a draft being ready.

A **deviation** is a change from the brief that keeps the page task, route, contribution and claim meanings intact, such as merging two short sections, changing a list to a table or leaving out a link whose destination failed. Record each one in section 6 with its reason. A change to the task, route, contribution or a claim's meaning is not a deviation. It goes back to B3 or B2.

## 6. Links, CTAs and assets

Use only the destinations the brief approves. Write anchors that describe the destination in the reader's words, placed where the reader would want to go there, and keep links apart so each is clear.

**Google guidance [C10]:** Google asks for anchor text that is descriptive, reasonably concise and relevant to both pages. It warns against generic anchors such as "click here" and against keyword stuffing in anchors, and says paid links should be qualified with `sponsored` or `nofollow`.

Write the CTA from the brief's concept and the verified destination and terms. Add no urgency, scarcity, guarantee or offer the dossier doesn't hold.

Mark each asset placement as `[Asset A01: purpose; caption direction]`. A caption describes only what the asset shows. Never describe a stock or generated image as a real job, product, person or place. Alt text is written at publishing, from the image's purpose on the page.

## 7. Revising existing copy

For named sections of an existing page, show the baseline text of each changed section under the new text, labelled `Baseline`, so reviewers can see what changed. Keep useful content, internal links and approved commercial meaning that the brief says to retain, and don't restyle sections outside scope.

For a substantial rewrite in full page mode, list the retained material in section 2 instead of repeating the whole old page. When content moves in from a consolidated page, keep its claim IDs and check that each claim still fits the destination's task.

## 8. AI assistance

This skill runs on an AI model. Anything the model or another AI tool produces that is not in the brief or dossier, whether a fact, figure, quote, source, entity, example or reader question, is unverified and stays out of the draft.

**Google guidance [C02]:** Google says generative AI can help with research and adding structure, that its outputs can contain inaccuracies, and that it is critical to fact-check and review all AI-generated content before publishing, including title elements, meta descriptions, structured data and image alt text. It suggests telling readers how content was created where that suits the audience, and Google's people-first questions ask whether the use of automation is evident to visitors through disclosures or in other ways [C01]. Whether and how the page discloses AI assistance is the business's decision. Record the brief's instruction in section 7.
